# Equilibrium

In the first iteration agents have no congestion information and assume **free flow**. When demand is assigned, the car overloads (it's the fastest). To avoid oscillations, the **Method of Successive Averages** is used:

$$
T_{\text{actuales}}^{*} = f \cdot T_{\text{nuevos}} + (1 - f) \cdot T_{\text{actuales}}, \qquad f = \frac{1}{it + 1}
$$

<OverleafRef sec="Bucle de equilibrio" />

The weight of new information decreases as $1/it$: the first iteration replaces fully, the second averages 50/50, the third weighs 1/3 and 2/3, etc.

## Interpretation

- Large $f$ in early iterations → fast adaptation (the city "learns")
- Small $f$ in late iterations → stabilization (the city "remembers")

## Stopping criterion

The loop stops when the residual

$$
r^{(it)} = \left\lVert T_{\text{new}}^{(it)} - T_{\text{current}}^{(it)} \right\rVert_{\infty}
$$

<OverleafRef sec="Criterio de Parada" tag="R-11" />

— the **undamped gap**: what the loaded network returns against the times the demand was computed with, the maximum over any mode and parcel — falls below the tolerance (**0.2 min**) for **two consecutive iterations** (robust to Monte Carlo noise). `max_iter` (150 by default, up to 300) remains as a safety cap: the default city converges in 10 iterations, and with 1 lane in 119, because the MSA's tail decays like $1/it$. If a run exhausts the cap, the status bar says so: what it shows is not an equilibrium.

## Coupled land ↔ transport loop

The outer loop (**City at equilibrium** page) alternates both modules to the joint fixed point: land use allocates households given accessibility $T_h$, that population runs the MSA, and the new times update $T_h$. Accessibility is the one of the Land use chapter — each stratum's mode-choice logsum, times the 44 trips of the month — now evaluated on the MSA's congested times instead of free flow:

$$
T_h^{\text{new}}(i) = -44 \cdot LS_h(i)
$$

<OverleafRef sec="Acoplamiento suelo–transporte" tag="R-12" />

with $LS_h(i)$ the logsum of stratum $h$ in cell $i$, averaged over those who own a car and those who do not. Between laps, $T_h \leftarrow \theta\, T_h^{\text{new}} + (1-\theta)\, T_h$, with a step $\theta$ that starts at 1 and halves every time the gap grows. The gap is measured in pesos, $\max_{h,i} \lvert T_h^{\text{new}}(i) - T_h(i) \rvert / \lambda_h$, in **\$/household-month**, with tolerance 100 \$/household-month: dividing by $\lambda_h$ makes it mean the same in the three strata. The coupled loop is declared converged only if its three parts converge — the outer gap, the MSA and the auction —; if the gap is met and another part is not, the loop stops anyway and reports it as not converged.

<Callout type="info">
Why one $T$ per stratum and not a common one? Each stratum chooses its mode with its own logit, so the same network yields it something different: the car, for instance, is owned by only part of each stratum. Until Sep-2026 accessibility was a time in minutes common to all, because per stratum it inverted the bid-rent. With the logsum turned into pesos by $\lambda_h$ — the same cost coefficient of the logit — that no longer happens, and the access difference between strata is part of the bid again (Land use chapter).
</Callout>

<CoupledFlowchart />

Each outer lap runs a full MSA, so it is expensive. In exchange, the map contracts strongly — the gap falls by about tenfold per lap — and a few laps suffice: more congestion downtown → households redistribute → congestion changes, less each time.
